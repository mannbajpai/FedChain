## Abstract

Federated fine-tuning lets several parties adapt a large language model without
pooling their data, but it leaves no evidence that the model a participant
receives was built from the updates the participants actually submitted. We
present FedChain, a federated LoRA fine-tuning system that anchors a SHA-256
commitment to every local and aggregated adapter on an EVM contract and moves
the adapters over content-addressed storage, with the aggregator re-deriving
each digest before an update enters the average.

The usual way to argue that such an audit layer is cheap is a statistically
insignificant accuracy difference. That is the weakest available form of the
claim. We instead report artefact equality: across two model scales, two data
partitions and three seeds, **162 of 162 client-adapter and 54 of 54
aggregated-model pairwise comparisons between audited and un-audited training
are bit-identical**, so the accuracy cost is exactly zero and no statistical
test is required. Anchoring adds no communication at all — the audited arm's
byte volume is identical to plain FedAvg — and gas is linear in participants
(`gas = 299,069 + 290,702·N`, R² = 0.999999 over N ∈ {1…100}) and flat across a
220× range of artefact sizes, because what is committed is 32 bytes regardless
of model size. Against four transport attacks the layer flags 50/50 at both
scales, and against a benign re-serialization control it flags 0/50, bounding
the false-positive rate at 5.8%. That control is the measurement behind the
paper's second contribution: an anchored commitment is only reproducible by a
third party if the adapter's configuration is hashed as canonical JSON rather
than as raw bytes.

## 1. Introduction

Federated learning lets organisations that cannot share data still train a
shared model, and parameter-efficient fine-tuning has made this practical for
large language models: a LoRA adapter is a few tens of megabytes against a base
model of several gigabytes, so a round of federated adaptation is a feasible
amount of traffic. What federated learning does not provide is *evidence*. A
participant hands the aggregator an update and later receives a global model.
Nothing in the protocol lets that participant, or a regulator, or a downstream
user, establish which updates went into it, or that the artefact retrieved from
storage is the artefact whose training was actually paid for.

A recurring proposal is to place commitments to the updates on a blockchain and
move the artefacts themselves over content-addressed storage. The design is
natural — the chain gives an append-only, independently readable record, and
content addressing makes retrieval self-verifying. The recurring difficulty is
evidential rather than architectural: papers in this line argue that the audit
layer is affordable by showing that accuracy differences between audited and
un-audited runs are not statistically significant. At the seed counts these
studies run, that argument is nearly unfalsifiable. An underpowered experiment
produces exactly the same non-significant difference as a correct
implementation, so the reader cannot distinguish "the audit layer is inert"
from "the experiment could not have detected it".

We take a different evidential standard. The audit layer in FedChain touches
transport and bookkeeping, never the learning math, so a correct implementation
should not produce a *small* difference — it should produce *no* difference,
down to the bytes. That is a checkable claim, and we check it.

**Contributions.**

1. **Bit-identity as the evaluation standard for audited FL.** We report
   SHA-256 equality over every trained artefact between the audited and
   un-audited arms — 162/162 client adapters and 54/54 aggregated models across
   two architectures and two partitions. The claim is exact, needs no
   statistical test, cannot be manufactured by an underpowered experiment, and
   is re-derivable by a reader from the released artefacts (§5.1).

2. **Canonical serialization is what makes an anchored commitment
   reproducible.** Hashing an adapter's raw bytes makes the on-chain digest
   irreproducible across honest re-runs, for a reason specific to how PEFT
   stores adapter configuration. We give the canonical-JSON construction that
   fixes it, and the benign-control column of our tamper experiment is the
   measurement that demonstrates it rather than an assertion that it works
   (§3.2, §5.3).

3. **A decomposition of audit cost in which anchoring is the free part.**
   On-chain anchoring adds *zero* communication: the chain-only arm's byte
   volume is identical to un-audited FedAvg. The entire +31.5–31.8% overhead
   arrives with content-addressed transport, which is an implementation choice
   rather than a cost of auditability. Gas is linear in participants and flat in
   artefact size (§5.2).

4. **A 2×2 over scale and skew with matched bounds.** The learning result is
   measured against isolated *and* pooled baselines on the same shards at a
   matched 4,500-update budget, with a partition-invariance control on the
   pooled arm (§5.4).

## 2. Related work

**Federated averaging and heterogeneity.** FedAvg [1] is the standard baseline
and the aggregation rule we audit. Label-skewed partitions drawn from a
Dirichlet distribution over class proportions [2] are the standard protocol for
the non-IID regime, and we use α = 0.3, a commonly reported operating point.
[VERIFY]

**Parameter-efficient fine-tuning.** LoRA [3] reparameterises a weight update
as a low-rank product, and QLoRA [4] combines it with 4-bit quantisation of the
frozen base model, which is what lets our study run end-to-end on a 4 GB
consumer GPU. We use the PEFT implementation [5]. Averaging the LoRA factors
`A` and `B` independently approximates averaging the effective updates `B·A`
and is standard in federated LoRA work; warm-starting each round from the
previous global adapter keeps the approximation tight. [VERIFY]

**Blockchain-anchored federated learning.** Systems in this line place update
commitments, incentives, or aggregation logic on-chain [6, 7, 8], generally
with an evaluation that reports accuracy parity against un-audited FL as a
non-significant difference and systems cost as a single aggregate overhead
figure. Our departure is evidential and analytical rather than architectural:
we report exact artefact equality in place of a significance test, and we
decompose the overhead so that the anchoring and transport components can be
costed separately. [VERIFY]

**Content-addressed storage.** IPFS [9] supplies the transport layer, and its
content identifiers make retrieval self-verifying with respect to the CID —
though, as §3.2 develops, self-verification with respect to a *reproducible*
commitment is a stronger requirement and needs work at the serialization layer.
[VERIFY]

**Generation metrics.** ROUGE-L [10] and BLEU [11] are reported as a
degeneration check only; §5.5 states the limits of what they support here.

## 3. Design

### 3.1 Protocol

A round proceeds in five stages. Stages 2–4 are the audit layer; removing them
leaves textbook FedAvg, and this is the ablation §5.1 measures.

1. **Train.** Each client `k` fine-tunes the current global adapter on its own
   shard for one local epoch, producing `θ_k`.
2. **Publish.** The adapter directory is packed reproducibly — sorted members,
   zeroed mtime/uid/gid — and pinned to IPFS, yielding a CID.
3. **Anchor.** The client submits `logUpdate(round, clientId, H(θ_k), cid)` to
   the `FedChainAudit` contract. The chain stores an append-only
   `AuditRecord{round, clientId, modelHash, ipfsCID, timestamp, submitter}`,
   indexed by round and by hash, and emits a `ModelLog` event with the round
   indexed for off-chain filtering. **The chain never stores weights.**
4. **Retrieve and verify.** The aggregator fetches each adapter by CID,
   recomputes `H(·)` over the retrieved bytes, and compares it against the
   on-chain commitment. A mismatch excludes that client from the round; a round
   in which no client survives verification aborts rather than producing an
   unsound global model.
5. **Aggregate.** FedAvg over the LoRA factors produces the new global adapter,
   which is anchored and pinned in turn.

Because the commitment is a digest and the artefact is content-addressed, an
external auditor who was not present for training can fetch any adapter by CID,
recompute the digest, and check it against the chain, without access to any
participant's data.

### 3.2 Canonical hashing, and why it is load-bearing

The obvious implementation of `H(·)` is SHA-256 over the adapter's raw bytes.
It does not work, for a reason that is easy to miss and fatal in exactly the
situation the system exists to serve.

PEFT stores an adapter's `target_modules` in a Python `set`, and Python salts
string hashes per process. Two honest runs that produce **bit-identical
weights** therefore serialise `adapter_config.json` with its keys and its
`target_modules` list in different orders. Under raw-byte hashing the two runs
yield different digests. The consequence is not a cosmetic instability: it means
an auditor who retrains from the same seed and the same data computes a digest
that disagrees with the anchored one and concludes the artefact was tampered
with. A commitment that honest re-execution cannot reproduce is not a
provenance mechanism.

FedChain's `sha256_directory` therefore folds each file's POSIX-normalised
relative path and its contents into the digest, iterating in sorted-path order
so the result is stable across filesystems and operating systems, and folds
`adapter_config.json` in as **canonical JSON** — sorted keys, sorted string
lists — rather than as raw bytes. Only the canonical PEFT checkpoint files are
included, so the commitment is not disturbed if the trainer drops incidental
files beside them. The digest then depends on the adapter's *content* and not
on incidental serialization order.

This is the kind of claim that is usually asserted. §5.3 measures it: the
benign control in the tamper experiment re-serialises `adapter_config.json`
with permuted key and list order and must **not** be flagged, and the
false-positive column is the evidence that canonicalisation works.

### 3.3 Threat model

**The aggregator is honest; transport is not.** A client anchors `H(θ_k)`
on-chain and publishes `θ_k` to content-addressed storage. An adversary who
controls the storage layer or the network path substitutes a different artefact
before retrieval. The adversary cannot rewrite the chain. This is the boundary
the commitment scheme is designed for, and §5.3 measures detection inside it.

Two things sit outside it by construction. A malicious client that anchors a
poisoned adapter it *genuinely trained* passes integrity checking, because the
hash matches — that is Byzantine robustness, orthogonal to provenance and
addressed by aggregation rules rather than by commitments. And anchoring a
digest leaks nothing about training data, but LoRA updates themselves are not
differentially private.

## 4. Experimental setup

**Models.** Two tiers, identical hyperparameters:
`HuggingFaceTB/SmolLM2-360M-Instruct` and `Qwen/Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct`. LoRA
adapters are 16.62 MiB and 16.83 MiB respectively.

### 4.1 Dataset

We fine-tune on **databricks-dolly-15k** [12], 15,011 instruction-following
records written by Databricks employees and released under CC BY-SA 3.0. Each
record is a quadruple of an `instruction`, an optional grounding `context`
passage, a `response`, and a human-assigned task `category`. 29.9% of the
training pool carries a context passage; instructions average 72 characters and
responses 356.

**Why this dataset.** Four properties made it the right choice for this study,
and the third is close to a requirement.

1. *Human-authored, not distilled.* Dolly was written by people rather than
   generated by a larger model, so the supervised target is not another
   system's output distribution and the fine-tuning signal is not a teacher
   model's artefacts.
2. *A realistic federated premise.* Instruction–response pairs drawn from
   internal knowledge work are exactly the kind of corpus an organisation
   cannot pool with a competitor's, so the "three parties who will not share
   data" framing is not a contrivance imposed on an otherwise-public dataset.
3. *A native task-label field.* The `category` field gives eight human-assigned
   task types, which is what makes the Dirichlet partition meaningful: our skew
   is defined over a real label that a practitioner would recognise, not over
   synthetic clusters invented to manufacture heterogeneity. A corpus without
   such a field would have forced us to label it ourselves, and the non-IID arm
   would then measure our labelling as much as the partition.
4. *A size that fits the budget.* 15k records at a 512-token cap let a full
   sweep — 2 tiers × 6 arms × 3 seeds, plus the matched skewed baselines — run
   to completion on a single 4 GB GPU. A larger corpus would have bought scale
   at the cost of the seeds and the second tier, and this paper's claims depend
   on repetition rather than on corpus size.

**Composition.** The training pool is dominated by open-ended question
answering, and the tail is thin:

| Category | Records | Share |
|---|---:|---:|
| open_qa | 3,614 | 24.9% |
| general_qa | 2,100 | 14.5% |
| classification | 2,071 | 14.3% |
| closed_qa | 1,720 | 11.9% |
| brainstorming | 1,705 | 11.7% |
| information_extraction | 1,461 | 10.1% |
| summarization | 1,153 | 7.9% |
| creative_writing | 687 | 4.7% |

**Representative records.** Three, one per structural type — grounded,
closed-form, and open-ended:

> **closed_qa** (grounded in a supplied passage)
> *instruction:* "When did Virgin Australia start operating?"
> *context:* "Virgin Australia … commenced services on 31 August 2000 as Virgin
> Blue, with two aircraft on a single route. …"
> *response:* "Virgin Australia commenced services on 31 August 2000 as Virgin
> Blue, with two aircraft on a single route."

> **classification** (no context; a one-token target)
> *instruction:* "Which is a species of fish? Tope or Rope"
> *response:* "Tope"

> **open_qa** (no context; free-form target)
> *instruction:* "Why can camels survive for long without water?"
> *response:* "Camels use the fat in their humps to keep them filled with
> energy and hydration for long periods of time."

**Split.** The first 500 records of the raw release are held out as a fixed
evaluation set *before* any shuffling, and are excluded from every client
shard; the remaining 14,511 are shuffled once and partitioned. The held-out
head turns out to track the training pool's category mix closely — the largest
deviation is general_qa at 18.2% against 14.5% — so it is not a biased slice
despite being taken by position rather than by stratification.

We checked the split for contamination rather than assuming it. Eight of the
500 evaluation records (1.6%) share an instruction string with some training
record, but seven of those carry a *different* response: they are independent
annotators answering the same generic prompt ("What is a verb?", "What is the
meaning of life?"). Exactly **one record in 500 (0.2%) is a full duplicate**.
Because every arm is scored on the same fixed evaluation set, this contributes
an identical constant to every arm and cannot bias a between-arm comparison,
which is what all of our claims are.

### 4.2 Partitions

Three clients. The IID partition assigns contiguous slices of the shuffled
pool, giving three equal 4,837-record shards whose category mixes agree to
within two points. The skewed partition draws, for each task category `c`,
client proportions `p_c ~ Dirichlet(α·1_N)` with α = 0.3 and deals that
category's records accordingly. The result (Fig. 1) is severe: client 2 holds
2,715 records of which 54% are brainstorming, 1% closed QA, and *zero* creative
writing, while client 3 holds 6,998 dominated by open QA and classification.

**Budget matching.** Arms are matched on two axes. *Updates:* 3 rounds × 3
clients × 500 samples = 4,500 for the federated arms; 1 round × 3 shards ×
1,500 = 4,500 for the pooled arm. *Data:* federated round `r` reads window
`[(r−1)·500, r·500)` of each shard, so three rounds cover `client_k[0:1500]`
and the union over `k` is 4,500 unique records — exactly what the pooled arm
sees. The second axis matters: with a fixed head slice every round, an `R`-round
federated run is `R` epochs over 500 examples and sees a third of the unique
data, and the measured "cost of federation" is then mostly a data-budget
artefact.

**Arms.** E0 local-only (federated schedule, aggregation disabled — the
isolation lower bound); E1 centralized on the pooled union (the upper bound);
E2 FedAvg with a trusted aggregator; E3 = E2 + on-chain anchoring; E4 = E3 +
IPFS transport with verified round-trip. The same E0/E1/E2 triple is run under
Dirichlet(0.3) as Ablation B1, which is what makes the learning claim sayable
under skew — a skewed federated arm compared against IID bounds would confound
partition with federation.

**Training.** QLoRA: 4-bit NF4 with double quantisation, bf16 compute; LoRA
r = 16, α = 32, dropout 0.05 on
`q_proj, k_proj, v_proj, o_proj, gate_proj, up_proj, down_proj`; lr 2e-4 with
cosine schedule and 0.03 warmup ratio; `paged_adamw_8bit`; gradient clipping at
0.3; micro-batch 1 with gradient accumulation 8; `max_seq_length` 512; gradient
checkpointing.

**Evaluation.** Token-weighted full-sequence NLL over the 500 held-out records,
so perplexity is not skewed by answer-length differences between adapters.
Generation metrics are recomputed on the finished adapters at 250 greedy
decodes with a single scorer (the HuggingFace `evaluate` backend), never from
per-run values.

**Statistics.** Seeds 42, 43, 44. All differences are paired per seed and then
averaged, never a difference of means — seed variance is shared between arms
and cancels. Intervals are 95% Student's *t*; at n = 3 the critical value is
**4.303**, not 1.96. Detection and false-positive rates get exact one-sided
Clopper–Pearson bounds.

**Infrastructure.** NVIDIA T600 Laptop GPU (4 GB), 12 CPU cores, WSL2;
PyTorch 2.5.1+cu121, Transformers 5.14.1, PEFT 0.20.0, TRL 1.9.2,
bitsandbytes 0.50.0, web3.py 7.16.0, Python 3.12.3. The chain is a Foundry
`anvil` devnet running the deployed `FedChainAudit` contract; storage is a
local Kubo IPFS node.

## 5. Results

### 5.1 The audit layer is an exact no-op

`[T2 — hash equality]`

For each (tier, partition) we compare the artefacts produced by the audited
arms against those produced by plain FedAvg, adapter by adapter, at both the
per-client and the aggregated level. Every comparison is exact SHA-256
equality over the canonical serialization of §3.2; the aggregated digest is
also the value written on-chain, so a reader can re-derive it from the released
artefacts.

**162 of 162 client-adapter comparisons and 54 of 54 aggregated-model
comparisons are identical**, across two architectures, two partitions and three
seeds. Validation loss differences are 0.000000 by construction rather than by
measurement. Adding on-chain anchoring, and then adding content-addressed
transport on top of it, changes nothing about what is learned.

A fourth instance of the same result falls out of the design without being
constructed for it: the fully audited skewed arm (E5) and the un-audited
skewed FedAvg arm (Ablation B1-E2) were run as separate experiments under
separate configurations, on different dates, and their validation losses agree
to six decimal places on all three seeds — `2.025167 / 2.024783 / 2.024699` at 360M and
`2.073369 / 2.072359 / 2.071123` at 0.5B.

We state this as equality, not as a non-significant difference. The distinction
is the point: a significance test at n = 3 would have been satisfied by an
implementation that perturbed training slightly, and the bit-level check would
not.

### 5.2 What the audit layer costs

`[T3 — systems cost]` `[Fig. 4]` `[Fig. 5]`

The cost decomposes, and the two halves behave very differently.

**Anchoring adds no communication whatsoever.** The chain-only arm moves
299.18 MiB at 360M and 302.86 MiB at 0.5B — byte-identical to un-audited
FedAvg, because a commitment is 32 bytes of digest plus a short identifier and
never carries weights. The entire **+31.5% (360M) / +31.8% (0.5B)** increase
arrives with content-addressed transport, and it is an artefact of the verified
round-trip rather than of auditability: the baseline accounts 18 adapter
transfers — 9 client uploads plus 9 global-broadcast legs, one per client per
round — sized from the directories on disk, whereas
the IPFS arm additionally accounts a **retrieval leg for every client
artefact** — the aggregator pulls each adapter back and re-hashes it before
averaging — plus one extra pinned copy of the global model, all measured on the
gzipped tar actually transferred, which is 0.79× the on-disk size (mean
13.11 MiB against 16.62 MiB). An operator who wants the audit trail without the
transport cost can run the chain-only configuration and pay nothing in bytes.

**Gas.** A full three-round run anchors 12 records — 9 client adapters and 3
aggregated models — for 2,997,464 gas with digests alone and 3,785,372 with
CIDs included, identically at both tiers. The 787,908 difference is the cost of
carrying the CID string, ≈65.7k gas per record.

**Wall-clock overhead is not distinguishable from zero at this seed count.**
The measured deltas are +0.2% and +0.7% at 360M and −0.2% and +0.2% at 0.5B —
the sign flips, and the per-arm intervals span hundreds of seconds against
means near 4,750 s. We therefore report a bound rather than a cost, and quote
the quantity that *is* measured cleanly: **mean integrity-check latency of
13.5 ms per artefact**, which is what the aggregator actually pays to verify a
commitment.

**Scaling.** Over N ∈ {1, 3, 5, 10, 25, 50, 100} participants, gas is linear:
`gas = 299,069 + 290,702·N`, R² = 0.999999, with N + 1 transactions per round
(one per client, one for the global model). Per-client cost amortises from
616,560 at N = 1 toward the 290,702 marginal cost, reaching 293,729 at N = 100.
Across a 220× range of artefact sizes (0.22 to 49.1 MiB) gas varies by
**0.0077%** — flat, because 32 bytes are committed at every size. This sweep
anchors digests and never loads a model, so it is model-independent by
construction and we report it once rather than per tier.

### 5.3 Tamper detection and the reproducibility control

`[T4 — tamper detection]` `[Fig. 6]`

Under the §3.3 threat model we run 50 trials of each of four transport attacks
and one benign control, at both tiers. `bitflip` perturbs stored bytes; `scale`
boosts the LoRA-B factor, i.e. substitutes a different model; `substitute`
swaps in another client's artefact; `replay` re-serves a previous round's
artefact.

All four attacks are flagged **50/50 at both tiers**, bounding the miss rate at
**≤5.8%** (one-sided 95%, 50 trials).

The benign control is the more interesting column. `reserialize` rewrites
`adapter_config.json` with its keys and its `target_modules` list in a
different order — byte-different, semantically identical, and exactly what a
second honest run produces for the reason given in §3.2. It is flagged
**0/50 at both tiers**, bounding the false-positive rate at **≤5.8%**. We
report the bound rather than "0%": 0 of 50 is not a zero rate, it is a rate no
larger than 5.8%.

That column is what makes the anchored commitment usable by a third party. Had
`H(·)` hashed raw bytes, this control would have been flagged at a high rate,
and every honest re-derivation of a commitment would have read as tampering.

### 5.4 Federation against matched bounds

`[T1 — main result]` `[Fig. 2]`

Between the isolation lower bound (E0) and the pooled upper bound (E1) lies the
headroom that federation is supposed to recover. We report the paired per-seed
gain `E0 − E2` and the fraction of headroom recovered, `(E0−E2)/(E0−E1)`,
computed per seed.

FedAvg improves on isolation in all four cells, with intervals excluding zero:
`0.00085 ± 0.00022` and `0.00530 ± 0.00057` at 360M under IID and Dirichlet
respectively, and `0.00964 ± 0.00093` and `0.01626 ± 0.00128` at 0.5B. In
headroom terms it recovers **2.4% ± 0.7%** and **12.7% ± 1.7%** at 360M, and
**34.0% ± 4.3%** and **41.5% ± 7.7%** at 0.5B. Every one of these is a
statement about a matched 4,500-update budget at R = 3.

The absolute gain is larger under skew at both scales — 6.21× at 360M and 1.69×
at 0.5B, with disjoint intervals in both cases. This is consistent with the
usual account, in which averaging supplies information a client cannot obtain
from its own shard and skew increases how much that is. We report it as a
direction at one α, not as a trend: two partitions is not a curve, and we make
no claim that the benefit is monotone in α or in scale.

**The partition is a redistribution, not a different task.** The pooled arm is
essentially unmoved by re-partitioning — 1.9884 against 1.9885 at 360M and
2.0499 against 2.0492 at 0.5B. This is the control that licenses reading the
E0 and E2 movements as effects of skew on *federation* rather than of the split
having changed the problem.

### 5.5 Generation quality

Recomputed on the finished adapters at 250 greedy decodes with a single scorer,
ROUGE-L and BLEU are reported as a degeneration check. At 0.5B under IID the
ordering matches the loss ordering (E1 0.2737, E2 0.2564, E0 0.2527 ROUGE-L);
in the remaining three cells the intervals overlap and separate nothing. No arm
collapses.

These intervals are **between-seed** at n = 3. Sample count controls the
Monte-Carlo error inside each seed's point estimate, not the seed-to-seed
spread the interval measures, so raising it buys a less noisy point estimate
per seed and not a narrower interval — separating generation metrics here needs
more seeds, not more decodes. The full table is released with the artefacts.

## 6. Limitations

**Fixed budget, not convergence.** Every loss curve is still descending at
R = 3 (Fig. 3) — at 0.5B, 2.1145 → 2.0792 → 2.0686, a per-round decay ratio
near 0.30.
The recovery fractions of §5.4 are properties of a 4,500-update budget; they are
not asymptotic statements, and they should not be read as the value of
federation at convergence.

**Seeds share a partition.** All three seeds read the same shards. Every
interval is therefore a training-noise interval, and it understates uncertainty
on results that depend on the partition draw.

**One α, label skew only.** The Dirichlet draw produces shards of 2,715 to
6,998 records, but the per-client, per-round cap means every client trains on
1,500 regardless, which holds the update budget equal and removes quantity skew
from the comparison. This is the standard Dirichlet benchmark and is weaker
than setups that let both quantity and labels skew.

**Small scale.** Two model tiers (360M, 0.5B), three clients, one dataset. The
learning result is a two-point comparison in scale, which is a direction and not
a law. The audit-layer results are size-independent by construction and we
expect them to transfer; the learning results we do not extrapolate.

**Devnet economics.** Gas figures are measured against real EVM execution and
transfer to a deployment. Devnet transaction latency does not — a production
deployment's confirmation time and fee market are outside this evaluation, and
the ≈2.99–3.79M gas per three-round run should be costed against a target L2
before it is read as a deployment budget.

**Out of scope by construction.** Byzantine robustness and privacy, as set out
in §3.3. A poisoned-but-honestly-anchored update passes integrity checking
because the hash matches; that is a property of the threat model, not a gap in
the implementation.

## 7. Conclusion

Verifiable provenance for federated LLM fine-tuning does not have to be argued
for with a significance test. We built the audit layer so that it cannot touch
the learning math, and then showed that it does not: every one of 162
client-adapter and 54 aggregated-model comparisons between audited and
un-audited training is bit-identical, across two scales, two partitions and
three seeds. The cost decomposes cleanly — anchoring is free in bytes, linear
in participants, and flat in model size; the +31.5% overhead belongs to
content-addressed transport and is optional. And the piece that makes the whole
scheme usable by a third party is a serialization detail: an anchored digest is
only a commitment if honest re-execution can reproduce it, which is why the
benign-control column of the tamper experiment is a result and not a formality.

## Figures

Rendered by `scripts/paper_figures.py` into `paper/figures/` as PDF (vector,
for the paper) and PNG at 300 dpi. Palette is Okabe-Ito, validated
colourblind-safe; fonts are serif with Type-42 embedding, sized for a
two-column template. Captions below are draft text — trim to fit.

**Fig. 1 — `fig1_heterogeneity`** *(full width, §4.2)*
> The two partitions of the Dolly training pool across three clients. Each cell
> is the share of that client's shard falling in that task category; shard sizes
> are in the row labels. The IID split gives three near-identical mixes; the
> Dirichlet(α=0.3) split does not — client 2 holds 54% brainstorming, 1% closed
> QA and no creative writing at all, and shard sizes range from 2,715 to 6,998.

**Fig. 2 — `fig2_gap_recovery`** *(full width, §5.4)* — **promote**
> Federated averaging against matched bounds, at a matched 4,500-update budget
> (R=3). Each row is one (model, partition) cell. The grey span is the headroom
> between the isolation bound E0 (square) and the pooled bound E1 (circle); the
> coloured segment is the part FedAvg (diamond, ±95% CI over 3 seeds) recovers,
> labelled as a percentage of the span. Lower loss is better, so the span is
> read right to left.

**Fig. 3 — `fig3_round_trajectory`** *(full width, §5.4/§6)* — **promote**
> Validation loss per federated round, IID, meaned over three seeds. E2, E3 and
> E4 produce bit-identical artefacts and are therefore one line. Every curve is
> still descending at R=3, so the recovery fractions in Table 1 are properties
> of the update budget and not of a converged system. Reference lines are the
> matched isolation (E0) and pooled (E1) arms. The skewed cells are omitted
> deliberately: they have their own bounds, and overlaying them on the IID
> references would confound partition with federation.

**Fig. 4 — `fig4_systems_cost`** *(full width, §5.2)*
> Cost of the audit layer, decomposed. Left: bytes crossing a participant
> boundary — on-chain anchoring (E3) is byte-identical to un-audited FedAvg, and
> the whole increase belongs to content-addressed transport (E4). Right: gas for
> the same three-round run. Anchoring is free in communication and communication
> is free in gas; the two costs do not mix.

**Fig. 5 — `fig5_gas_scaling`** *(full width, §5.2)*
> Anchoring cost against federation size and artefact size, at N+1 transactions
> per round. Left: gas is linear in participants over N ∈ {1…100}. Right: gas is
> flat across a 220× range of artefact sizes, because 32 bytes are committed at
> every size. One tier is plotted — the sweep anchors digests and never loads a
> model, so both tiers produce identical numbers by construction.

**Fig. 6 — `fig6_tamper_detection`** *(full width, §5.3)*
> Integrity checking under the transport threat model, 50 trials per cell per
> tier. Four attacks are flagged 50/50 and the benign re-serialization control
> 0/50, at both scales. Bars would encode nothing here — every outcome is 0 or
> 100 — so the figure plots the exact one-sided 95% Clopper–Pearson interval,
> which is the actual content: miss rate ≤5.8%, false-positive rate ≤5.8%.

**If only two figures fit**, take Fig. 2 and Fig. 3: the first is the learning
